How much of my RRSP / RRIF should I withdraw this year?

A mortality-based withdrawal rate for tax-deferred savings (RRSP, RRIF, other registered accounts). Unlike the fixed “4% rule”, the percentage rises with age and reaches 100% at age 100, because less and less lifetime remains to spread the money over.

How this is calculated

Each year the suggested withdrawal is the balance divided by the survival-weighted present value of one dollar a year for the rest of your life: p = 1 / Σ St · (1+r)−t, where St is the chance of being alive t years from now (from the Statistics Canada life tables used on the mortality page) and r is your expected after-inflation return. Withdrawing p of the remaining balance each year spends the money evenly (in today’s dollars) over your expected lifetime. At 100 the rate is 100% because the model has nothing left to plan for. The cautious options instead assume you live until the age at which only 25% or 10% of people your age are still alive.

The “expected balance” column assumes you follow the suggestion and earn exactly your expected return every year; real returns vary, so recompute each year with your actual balance. Dollar figures are in today’s purchasing power.

The RRIF minimum is the federal schedule: 1/(90 − age) before 71, a fixed table from 71, and 20% from 95. It only applies once an RRSP is converted to a RRIF (required by the end of the year you turn 71). Larger withdrawals are taxed as income and can push you into a higher bracket, and any remaining RRSP/RRIF is generally taxed as income on death (unless it rolls over to a spouse), which is the reason for drawing down registered money first.
